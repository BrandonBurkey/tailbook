# The 24-Month Items Owners Forget

Every owner knows when the annual is due. The items that catch owners out come around so rarely you never build a habit, right up until the day you file IFR, sell the airplane, or file an insurance claim. Here are the long-interval items worth putting on your calendar today, with the regulations behind them.

## First, the math: what a "calendar month" means

Most of these intervals are written in *calendar months*, which aren't the same as plain months. The common reading, reflected in FAA guidance, is that the interval runs to the **last day of the month** in which it comes due. An inspection signed off on March 3, 2026 with a 12-calendar-month interval is good through **March 31, 2027**, not March 3.

"About two years ago" is not a due date. Write down the month.

## 1. The transponder check: 24 calendar months, VFR too

**14 CFR 91.413** says you may not use an ATC transponder unless, within the preceding 24 calendar months, it has been tested and inspected and found to comply with Appendix F of Part 43. The same test is required after any installation or maintenance that could introduce data-correspondence errors.

It's often mistaken for an IFR item. It isn't: the rule turns on *using the transponder*, and most VFR owners use theirs on every flight.

## 2. The altimeter and static system: 24 calendar months, for IFR

**14 CFR 91.411** requires that, to fly IFR in controlled airspace, the static pressure system, each altimeter, and the automatic pressure altitude reporting system have been tested and inspected within the preceding 24 calendar months (Part 43 Appendix E, plus Appendix F for the altitude-reporting side).

Three things owners miss:

- **It's an operational rule.** A VFR-only airplane with a lapsed 91.411 check is fine for VFR, but the day you pick up an IFR clearance, it isn't.
- **It's required again after the static system is opened and closed** (other than the system drain and alternate static valves). An avionics job or a panel upgrade can restart the clock.
- **You're not approved above the altitude you were tested to.** 91.411(d) limits IFR operation to the maximum altitude to which all altimeters and the altitude-reporting system were tested.

Shops usually do this test and the transponder check in one visit, so the two tend to expire together.

## 3. The ELT: two clocks, not one

**14 CFR 91.207** gives your emergency locator transmitter two separate deadlines.

- **Inspection:** within 12 calendar months of the last one, covering proper installation, battery corrosion, operation of the controls and crash sensor, and a sufficient signal from the antenna (91.207(d)). It's often done at the annual, but it's a separate requirement with its own entry.
- **Battery:** replace (or recharge, if rechargeable) when the transmitter has been in use for more than **one cumulative hour**, or when **50 percent of the battery's useful life** (as established by the transmitter manufacturer) has expired (91.207(c)). The new expiration date must be marked on the outside of the transmitter and entered in the maintenance record.

That label, often buried in the tailcone, is the item owners almost never look at. Ask your shop to read it to you. If your ELT went off in the hangar for an afternoon, tell your IA, because the one-cumulative-hour rule may apply.

(91.207(f) exempts some operations, such as aircraft equipped to carry only one person and certain training flights within 50 nm of the departure airport, so check the list if you think it applies.)

## 4. The registration: now an 84-month item

Since January 23, 2023, a Certificate of Aircraft Registration lasts **seven years** instead of three (**14 CFR 47.40**). The certificate expires seven years after the last day of the month in which it was issued, and you can renew during the six months before it expires.

Seven years is long enough to make the registration the most forgettable item of all. Check your expiration date in seconds on the FAA Registry's N-number inquiry. Because the registration certificate must be on board (91.203), an expired one is not a paperwork footnote.

## 5. The VOR check: 30 days, and it's on you

If you use VORs under IFR, **14 CFR 91.171** requires an operational check within the preceding 30 days, within ±4° on a ground check or VOT and ±6° on an airborne check. The person doing the check logs the date, place and bearing error, and signs. It's a pilot item, so it lives in your records, not your mechanic's.

## Why it matters beyond the ramp check

Two reasons, both financial.

**Insurance.** Aviation insurance writers have long cautioned that coverage can be denied when the aircraft was out of inspection, and that includes the pitot-static and transponder checks, not only the annual. Read your policy's airworthiness language.

**Resale.** Buyers and pre-buy inspectors read the logbooks. The minimum is to keep records of required inspections until the work is repeated or superseded, or for one year (91.417(b)), but buyers prefer owners who keep everything. A clean, complete trail of these "small" checks shows an owner who paid attention.

## A five-minute fix

Pull your logbooks tonight and write down five dates: transponder, static/altimeter, ELT inspection, ELT battery (from the label), and registration expiration. Convert each to the end of its due month. Put them in a calendar with reminders 60, 30 and 7 days before. Then hand the list to your IA at your next annual. They, and you as pilot in command, make the airworthiness call. The list just makes sure nothing comes due without warning.
